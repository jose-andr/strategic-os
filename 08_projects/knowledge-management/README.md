# Knowledge Management

## Status

Active

## Purpose

Knowledge Management strengthens how service and customer-related information is created, owned, maintained, found and reused across the customer experience environment.

The project is not primarily about introducing a knowledge-management platform.

Its strategic role is to establish trustworthy information flows so that customers, staff, channels and enabling technologies can access sufficiently accurate, current and usable service knowledge.

This creates operational value today while strengthening the information foundations required for connected interactions, search, automation and future AI-enabled assistance.

## Strategic question

> How do we create trusted, maintainable service knowledge that can flow reliably between service owners, frontline staff, customer channels and enabling technologies?

## Problem framing

Service information is often distributed across:

* service-owner knowledge;
* CRM content;
* websites;
* contact-centre guidance;
* internal documentation;
* customer-response templates;
* service directories;
* digital assistants;
* workflow systems; and
* informal staff knowledge.

The central problem is not simply that information exists in multiple places.

The strategic problem is whether the organisation can reliably determine:

* what the authoritative information is;
* who owns it;
* where it should be maintained;
* how changes propagate;
* whether customer-facing language remains consistent;
* whether staff can find the right information;
* whether channels are using compatible service definitions; and
* whether machines can safely retrieve and use the information.

Without those conditions, introducing another repository, search tool or AI capability may increase complexity rather than improve service.

## Desired outcome

Establish a practical Knowledge Management capability that:

* clarifies authoritative service-information sources;
* improves ownership and information currency;
* reduces inconsistent or duplicated guidance;
* improves findability for staff and customers;
* supports consistent information across channels;
* makes service information more reusable;
* improves handover and interaction continuity;
* strengthens governance;
* reduces recurring manual reconciliation; and
* creates stronger foundations for search, automation and AI.

## Scope

The primary focus is information needed to understand and deliver customer services.

This may include:

* service descriptions;
* eligibility and requirements;
* customer intents;
* service pathways;
* channel offers;
* contact reasons;
* routing guidance;
* customer-facing instructions;
* service status information;
* escalation guidance;
* frequently used operational knowledge;
* service-owner information; and
* structured knowledge required by digital or assisted channels.

The project does not assume that every branch document should become part of the customer-service knowledge layer.

Branch-wide working documentation is addressed separately through the Branch Documentation Framework.

## Capability model

### 1. Source-of-truth clarity

Establish clear rules for determining:

* which system or location is authoritative for a type of information;
* which copies are derived or presentation layers;
* who owns the authoritative content;
* how users can identify trusted information; and
* what happens when sources conflict.

The objective is not necessarily to place all knowledge in one repository.

The objective is to make authority and relationships between sources understandable.

### 2. Information ownership

Define practical ownership for service knowledge.

Ownership should clarify:

* who is accountable for accuracy;
* who can make changes;
* who validates material updates;
* who monitors currency;
* how ownership transfers when responsibilities change; and
* how unresolved ownership issues are escalated.

Knowledge without sustainable ownership should not be treated as reliably governed knowledge.

### 3. Information quality

Develop repeatable approaches for assessing:

* accuracy;
* completeness;
* currency;
* consistency;
* usability;
* customer language;
* accessibility;
* appropriate level of detail; and
* suitability for reuse across channels.

Quality should be assessed in relation to the information's intended use rather than through documentation volume alone.

### 4. Information flow

Understand how service information moves from its source to the people and channels that depend on it.

A typical information flow may include:

**Service owner → authoritative source → knowledge layer → staff or channel → customer**

The project should identify where this flow breaks down through:

* manual copying;
* delayed updates;
* inconsistent interpretation;
* disconnected systems;
* unclear handoffs;
* duplicate maintenance;
* missing ownership; or
* channel-specific versions of the same service logic.

The objective is to reduce unnecessary transformations between authoritative knowledge and its use.

### 5. Service information architecture

Structure information around customer and service needs rather than only organisational structures.

Relevant patterns may include:

* customer intent;
* service offer;
* support reason;
* service pathway;
* response mode;
* routing logic;
* status;
* required information;
* channel availability; and
* escalation.

This structure should help information become reusable across human and digital service contexts.

### 6. Findability and retrieval

Improve the ability of staff, customers and approved systems to retrieve relevant information.

This includes understanding:

* how people search;
* terminology differences;
* metadata;
* taxonomy;
* navigation;
* search behaviour;
* common failure points; and
* whether users can identify the authoritative answer.

Findability should not be treated as a search-engine problem alone.

Poor source information, weak ownership and inconsistent structure cannot be fully solved through better search.

### 7. Governance and lifecycle

Knowledge requires a sustainable lifecycle.

The capability should consider:

* creation;
* approval;
* publication;
* maintenance;
* review;
* change;
* retirement;
* records obligations;
* access;
* privacy;
* security; and
* accountability.

Governance should be proportionate to the risk and value of the information.

The objective is controlled usefulness, not documentation bureaucracy.

## Viability argument

The viability case should begin with the information environment already in use.

The first objective is not to create a new central repository.

It is to understand:

* existing authoritative sources;
* current information flows;
* ownership;
* duplication;
* maintenance effort;
* recurring failure points; and
* where clearer rules or structures can reduce operational burden.

Existing organisational systems should remain systems of record where appropriate.

Additional technology should be introduced only where it materially improves:

* findability;
* reuse;
* governance;
* consistency;
* automation; or
* maintenance effort.

The preferred approach is:

> Reduce ambiguity and recurring maintenance before adding another knowledge layer.

## AI argumentation

Knowledge Management has a direct relationship with AI, but the business case should not begin with AI.

AI systems that search, summarise, retrieve or generate service information depend on the quality of the sources available to them.

If underlying knowledge is:

* outdated;
* contradictory;
* poorly owned;
* duplicated;
* inaccessible;
* ambiguously structured; or
* mixed with uncontrolled material,

AI can return confident but unreliable outputs.

The appropriate sequence is therefore:

**Trusted service knowledge → reliable retrieval → consistent service context → automation and AI potential**

AI readiness is primarily a knowledge-quality, information-architecture and governance problem before it becomes a model-selection problem.

## Retrieval versus generation

For service information, a useful distinction is:

### Retrieval

Find and present trusted information from approved sources.

Examples may include:

* locating service guidance;
* identifying relevant procedures;
* retrieving service requirements;
* finding customer-response information; or
* summarising an approved source.

### Generation

Create new wording or interpretation based on available information.

Examples may include:

* drafting a response;
* producing a summary;
* adapting language for a customer;
* synthesising several sources; or
* suggesting a next action.

Retrieval generally presents less information risk than unconstrained generation, but both still depend on source quality, permissions and human oversight.

AI-enabled service use cases should therefore identify which behaviour is required.

## AI readiness questions

Before proposing AI use, ask:

* What is the authoritative source?
* Is ownership clear?
* Is the information current?
* Can the source be traced?
* Are conflicting versions present?
* Is the information appropriately structured?
* Can the intended user or system access it?
* Are privacy, records and security requirements understood?
* Is retrieval sufficient, or is generation actually required?
* What happens when the system cannot find a reliable answer?
* Is human review required?
* How will incorrect outputs be detected?
* Does the solution reduce recurring work?
* Who will maintain the AI capability and its source connections?

## Business value and AI decision rule

Do not implement AI merely because service information is available.

Pursue an AI-enabled knowledge use case when:

1. a material customer or staff problem exists;
2. trusted source information exists;
3. ownership and governance are sufficiently clear;
4. retrieval or generation provides meaningful value;
5. permissions and privacy support the use;
6. failure can be detected and managed;
7. human oversight matches the level of risk; and
8. the capability removes more recurring administration than it creates.

## Strategic OS interpretation

The reusable principle emerging from this project is:

> AI cannot compensate sustainably for weak organisational knowledge foundations.

A useful progression is:

**Source → Structure → Flow → Retrieval → Intelligence**

For Knowledge Management:

**Trusted source → usable service structure → reliable information flow → effective retrieval → AI potential**

This complements the Customer Accounts Management progression:

**Trusted accounts → reliable customer context → connected experience → automation and AI potential**

Together they create two different forms of trusted context:

* **Customer Accounts Management** provides information about the customer.
* **Knowledge Management** provides information about the service.

Connected customer interactions increasingly depend on both.

## Information-flow perspective

Knowledge should be viewed as a flow rather than only as a collection of documents.

A useful system view is:

**Service definition
→ authoritative information
→ structured knowledge
→ channel or staff access
→ customer interaction
→ feedback or change signal
→ service-information update**

The quality of the overall service depends on the reliability of that loop.

The project should therefore identify where:

* information is manually duplicated;
* changes fail to propagate;
* staff create workarounds;
* channels diverge;
* feedback does not reach information owners; or
* knowledge becomes stale.

These are service-design and systems problems as much as documentation problems.

### Branch Documentation Framework

Knowledge Management and branch documentation are related but should remain distinct.

Knowledge Management focuses primarily on service and customer-related knowledge required to support interactions and service delivery.

The Branch Documentation Framework focuses on how internal branch knowledge, decisions, working practices and reusable organisational documentation are structured and governed.

The boundary should be validated through use rather than assumed to be absolute.

## Current decisions

* Treat Knowledge Management as an information-flow capability rather than a repository project.
* Lead with current customer, staff and organisational value.
* Treat technology selection as downstream of information and governance needs.
* Preserve organisational systems of record.
* Prefer authoritative sources and links over duplicated information.
* Treat service information architecture as part of the capability.
* Position AI readiness as a consequence of trusted, governed knowledge.
* Prefer retrieval from trusted sources over unconstrained generation where the business need allows.
* Avoid introducing technology that creates more recurring maintenance than it removes.
